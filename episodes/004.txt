# Food and Farm
SONIA: Good morning, and welcome to English Radio. I'm Sonia.
RYAN: And I'm Ryan. Thanks for tuning in on your way to work.
SONIA: Ryan, earlier in the series we talked about how dairy cows are milked and monitored. Today, I want to find out what happens to some of that milk next.
RYAN: Let me guess. Cheese?
SONIA: Cheese. One of humanity's greatest inventions, in my opinion.
RYAN: I won't argue with that.
SONIA: Our guest today is Connor, who makes cheese on his family's dairy farm in County Cork, in the south of Ireland. Connor, welcome to the show.
CONNOR: Thanks a million, Sonia. It's great to be here.
RYAN: Connor, let's start at the very beginning. How does milk turn into cheese?
CONNOR: Well, in a way, it's very simple. Cheese has only four main [[ingredients|ingredient]]: milk, salt, a starter culture, and something called rennet. But what you do with those four things, and how carefully you do it, makes all the difference.
SONIA: What's a starter culture?
CONNOR: It's a mixture of friendly [[bacteria]]. We add them to warm milk, and they start eating the natural sugar in the milk, which is called lactose. As they eat it, they produce acid, and the milk slowly becomes sour.
RYAN: Sour milk doesn't sound very appetising.
CONNOR: Not on its own. But that acid is where a lot of the [[flavour]] comes from. Then we add the rennet. Rennet contains an enzyme that makes the milk set, a bit like a jelly. After about half an hour, you've got a big soft block in the vat.
SONIA: And then what?
CONNOR: Then we cut it into small pieces with long knives, and the liquid starts to come out. The solid pieces are called curds, and the liquid is called whey.
RYAN: Curds and whey. Like the old nursery rhyme.
CONNOR: Exactly. Little Miss Muffet, sat on a tuffet, eating her curds and whey. Most people say that rhyme as a child without having any idea what curds and whey are.
SONIA: I certainly didn't.
CONNOR: So, the curds become the cheese, and the whey is drained away. On our farm, we actually feed the whey to our pigs, so nothing is wasted. Then we put the curds into moulds, press them, add salt, and leave them to age.
RYAN: Where does the rennet come from?
CONNOR: Traditionally, it came from the stomach of a young calf. That was how cheese was made for thousands of years. Nowadays, most cheese is made with rennet that's produced by microbes, so it's suitable for vegetarians. But some [[traditional]] cheeses still use animal rennet.
SONIA: How much milk do you need to make a cheese?
CONNOR: A lot more than people think. For a hard cheese, like a cheddar, you need about ten litres of milk to make one kilo of cheese.
RYAN: Ten litres for one kilo! So a big wheel of cheese represents an awful lot of milking.
CONNOR: It does. That's why I always tell people, when you eat a slice of cheese, you're eating a lot of grass and a lot of hard work.
SONIA: Speaking of grass, I've heard that Irish dairy is a bit special.
CONNOR: It is, and we're very proud of it. In Ireland, the climate is mild and wet, so grass grows for most of the year. Our cows spend much of the year outside, grazing in the fields. That gives the milk a lovely rich colour, especially the butter, which is quite yellow.
RYAN: Why does grass make it yellow?
CONNOR: Fresh grass contains beta-carotene, the same natural colour you find in carrots. The cows eat it, and some of it ends up in the fat of the milk. So you can actually see the season in the cheese. Cheese made in summer, when the grass is lush, is often more yellow than cheese made in winter.
SONIA: That's lovely. So the cheese tells you when it was made.
CONNOR: It does. Cheese is like a diary of the farm.
RYAN: Let's talk about ageing. Why do some cheeses need to wait for months, or even years?
CONNOR: Because ageing is when the real magic happens. Inside the cheese, the bacteria and enzymes keep working slowly, breaking down proteins and fats into hundreds of different flavour compounds. A young cheddar is mild and creamy. A cheddar that's two years old is sharp and complex, and it can even have little crunchy crystals in it.
SONIA: Oh, I love those crunchy bits!
CONNOR: Everyone does. A lot of people think they're salt, but they're actually crystals of an amino acid. They're a sign of a well-aged cheese.
RYAN: Where do you keep the cheese while it's ageing?
CONNOR: In a cool, damp room, which we call the cave, although ours is really just a building with a very good air conditioner. Real caves used to be perfect, because they stay the same temperature all year. In fact, the name cheddar comes from a village in Somerset, in England, called Cheddar, where there are big limestone caves. People used to store cheese in them.
SONIA: And can any cheese be called cheddar?
CONNOR: Yes, cheddar is made all over the world now, from England to New Zealand. But some cheeses are protected. Take Stilton, the famous English blue cheese. It can only be made in three English counties, using a specific method.
RYAN: Is it made in the village of Stilton?
CONNOR: That's the funny thing. No, it isn't. The village of Stilton is in a different county, so it's not allowed to make Stilton cheese.
SONIA: That's ridiculous. And very British.
CONNOR: It is a bit. The name comes from the village because that's where the cheese was sold to travellers, a long time ago.
RYAN: How do you get the blue in a blue cheese?
CONNOR: With a mould, a type of Penicillium, which is a relative of the mould used to make penicillin. We add it to the milk, and after a few weeks we push long steel needles into the cheese, to let air in. The mould grows along those little tunnels, and that's where you see the blue lines.
SONIA: I have to say, when you describe it like that, it sounds a bit strange.
CONNOR: It does! Cheese is basically controlled decay. But delicious controlled decay.
RYAN: I'm going to remember that phrase. Now, Connor, I know some listeners can't drink milk easily. Can they eat cheese?
CONNOR: Often, yes. People who are lactose intolerant can't easily [[digest]] lactose, the milk sugar. But in hard, aged cheeses, the bacteria have eaten almost all the lactose. A mature cheddar or a parmesan contains very little. So many people who can't drink a glass of milk can enjoy a piece of hard cheese. Of course, everyone's different, so it's best to check with a doctor.
SONIA: What about making cheese at home? Is it possible?
CONNOR: Absolutely. Simple fresh cheeses, like ricotta or paneer, are very easy. You heat milk, add some lemon juice or vinegar, and the milk separates into curds and whey. Drain it through a cloth, and you've got cheese in half an hour.
RYAN: That sounds like a nice weekend project.
CONNOR: It's a great one to do with children. Hard cheeses are more difficult. You need good hygiene, the right temperature, and a lot of patience.
SONIA: Connor, cheese is made all over the world. Do you have any favourites from other countries?
CONNOR: Oh, so many. From Italy, I love real mozzarella, made from the milk of water buffalo. It's soft, wet and wonderfully fresh. From Greece, there's feta, which is traditionally made from sheep's milk, or a mixture of sheep's and goat's milk. And from Cyprus, there's halloumi, which you can grill or fry without it melting.
RYAN: Halloumi is the squeaky one, isn't it?
CONNOR: That's the one. It squeaks against your teeth when you bite it. Children love it.
SONIA: And are those names protected too, like Stilton?
CONNOR: Some of them, yes. In Europe, only cheese made in Greece in the traditional way can be sold as feta. It's a bit like champagne, which has to come from the Champagne region of France. The idea is to protect the [[cultural heritage]] and the farmers of a particular place.
RYAN: I have to ask you about something very strange that happens in England. Have you heard of cheese rolling?
CONNOR: Oh, I have! It's mad. Every year, in Gloucestershire, people chase a big round cheese down an extremely steep hill. The first person to reach the bottom wins the cheese.
SONIA: And people get hurt, don't they?
CONNOR: Every year! The hill is so steep that nobody can really run down it. They just fall, roll and bounce all the way to the bottom. There are first aid teams waiting.
RYAN: All for a cheese.
CONNOR: All for a cheese. Although to be fair, it's a very good cheese. It's a Double Gloucester, a local hard cheese.
SONIA: Would you ever enter?
CONNOR: Absolutely not. I prefer to make cheese, not chase it.
SONIA: Connor, why did you decide to make cheese, rather than just selling your milk?
CONNOR: That's a good question. My parents sold all their milk to the big dairy processor, like most farms in Ireland. But milk prices go up and down, and a small farm has very little control. When I came back to the farm after college, I wanted to add value. If you sell milk, you get a price per litre. If you turn that milk into a [[handmade]] cheese, and sell it at farmers' markets and to restaurants, you can earn much more from the same milk.
RYAN: So it's a business decision as well as a passion.
CONNOR: Exactly. And I also love the connection with people. At the market, someone tastes my cheese and says, oh, that's gorgeous. You don't get that when your milk goes into a lorry.
SONIA: Have you noticed any trends in what customers want?
CONNOR: People really care about where their food comes from now. They want something [[authentic]], with a story. They want to know the name of the farm and sometimes even the names of the cows.
RYAN: Do your cows have names?
CONNOR: Of course they do. There's Daisy, Primrose, Bridget, and a very bossy one called Maureen.
SONIA: I love that. Do you ever export your cheese?
CONNOR: A little. Some goes to Britain, and we've had interest from Japan, actually. I visited Hokkaido a couple of years ago, because a lot of Japanese cheese is made there. The farmers were fascinated by our grass-based system, and I was fascinated by how carefully they did everything.
RYAN: Connor, final question. What's your favourite way to eat cheese?
CONNOR: Honestly? A slice of mature cheddar on a piece of good brown bread, with a cup of tea. Simple things are the best.
SONIA: Tea again! It always comes back to tea on this show.
CONNOR: Sure, it's the same in Ireland. Tea with everything.
RYAN: Connor, thank you so much.
CONNOR: Thank you both. It was a pleasure.

# The Report
RYAN: Now it's time for The Report, and today we're changing the subject completely. Sonia?
SONIA: Thanks, Ryan. In late two thousand and twenty, an unusual song climbed to the top of global viral charts on music streaming services. It wasn't a new hit by an American pop star. It was a Japanese song from nineteen seventy-nine, sung by Miki Matsubara, called Stay With Me.
SONIA: The song is part of a style known as city pop, which was popular in Japan in the late nineteen seventies and the nineteen eighties. City pop mixes Japanese pop with American influences such as soft rock, funk, jazz and disco. The songs are smooth and stylish, and many of them are about city life, romance and summer evenings.
SONIA: City pop grew up at a time when Japan's economy was booming. People had money to spend on new technology, such as portable cassette players and car stereos. Many songs feel made for driving along a coastal road at sunset, which is why some fans describe the music as the sound of a confident, optimistic Japan.
SONIA: For decades, city pop was mostly forgotten outside Japan. That changed in the late twenty-tens, when another song, Plastic Love by Mariya Takeuchi, began to spread on YouTube. Its popularity was partly an accident. The website's recommendation algorithm kept suggesting the video to listeners all over the world, and millions of people clicked on it.
SONIA: After that, the trend snowballed. Young people in America, Europe, Indonesia and South Korea began searching for more city pop. Short clips of the songs [[went viral|go viral]] on social media, and DJs and producers sampled the music in new tracks.
SONIA: Why does the music appeal to a [[generation]] who weren't even born in the nineteen eighties? Some critics say it's because the songs feel [[nostalgic]] for a time that listeners never experienced, a kind of imagined past where everything seemed exciting and full of possibility. Others say it's simpler than that: the songs are just very well made, with skilled musicians and catchy melodies.
SONIA: The revival has had real effects in Japan, too. Record companies have reissued old albums on vinyl, and second-hand record shops in Tokyo now welcome foreign visitors looking for original copies. Some of the artists, now in their sixties and seventies, have found a new audience they never expected.
SONIA: However, not everyone is comfortable with the trend. Some Japanese listeners point out that city pop was only one part of a much richer music scene, and they worry that international fans see a narrow, stereotyped picture of Japanese culture.
SONIA: Still, the story shows how the internet can give old art a second life. A song recorded more than forty years ago, on the other side of the world, can suddenly find millions of new fans, thanks to a recommendation on a screen. That's the report for today.
RYAN: Thanks, Sonia. I actually listened to some city pop while I was getting ready this morning. It's very good driving music.
SONIA: It really is. Perfect for the commute.
