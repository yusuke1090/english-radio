# NEPA Took Light
[amb off]
[bgm lofi.187 0.1]
ADA: Okay, first, small problem. Can you hear that noise? That rumbling?
ADA: That's my neighbour's generator. Because, as usual, NEPA has taken light.
ADA: Let me explain for my international people. In Nigeria, the electricity is... not [[reliable]]. We have [[power cuts|power cut]] all the time. Sometimes for an hour. Sometimes for a whole day. The national electricity company used to be called NEPA, a long time ago. It's had different names since then, but everybody still says "NEPA". So when the power goes off, we say "NEPA has taken light". And when it comes back, the whole street shouts "Up NEPA!" Like we scored a goal.
ADA: So almost everybody who can afford one has a generator. Small ones, big ones, all running on petrol or diesel. The whole city hums. It's noisy, it's [[expensive]], it's bad for the air. But that's life here. We adapt. Lagosians are experts at adapting. If you can survive Lagos, you can survive anywhere.
ADA: Anyway. My phone is charged, my camera is charged, my power bank is charged. Ada is prepared. Let's go and find a danfo.

# The Danfo
[bgm afro.188 0.12]
ADA: Okay, so this is a danfo. You've probably seen them in photos. These yellow minibuses, with the black stripes, are the heart of Lagos transport. They go everywhere. They're cheap. They're crowded. They have no fixed timetable. And they are driven by men who believe the rules of the road are just suggestions.
CONDUCTOR (shout): Obalende! Obalende! CMS! Enter with your change o! Enter with your change!
ADA: That's the conductor. Every danfo has one. He hangs out of the open door, shouting the [[destinations|destination]], collecting the money, and arguing with everybody. He's saying we're going to Obalende, on the Island, and that we should have the exact money. Because if you give him a big note, he will not have change. He will never have change. He has never, in the history of Lagos, had change.
ADA: Okay, we're in. I'm squeezed between a woman with a huge bag of tomatoes and a man in a suit reading his Bible. The seat is meant for three people. There are five of us. This is normal.
CONDUCTOR (shout): Owa! Owa! Move, move! Shift small, madam!
ADA: "Shift small" means move over a little bit. It's the most-used phrase in Lagos transport. Even when there's no space left to shift into, you shift small.
ADA: Lagos is a [[metropolis]]. It's the biggest city in Nigeria, and one of the biggest in Africa. Nobody knows exactly how many people live here. Some say fifteen million, some say over twenty. It's growing so fast that nobody can count. Every day, people arrive from every part of the country, looking for work, looking for [[opportunity]]. That's [[urbanisation]], my people. Live, in real time, in a yellow bus.

# Third Mainland Bridge
[amb wind 0.4]
[bgm citypop.189 0.12]
ADA: Okay, look out of the window. This is the Third Mainland Bridge. It's one of the longest bridges in Africa. Almost twelve kilometres. It goes right across the lagoon, from the Mainland, where most people live, to the Island, where a lot of the offices and the business are.
ADA: On one side, you can see the water. And on the water, look, there's Makoko. Houses built on wooden poles, standing in the lagoon, with people moving between them in canoes. A whole [[community]] on water. Very beautiful from the bridge. A very hard life, if you live there.
ADA: And on a weekday morning, this bridge is one of the worst [[traffic jams|traffic jam]] on earth. We call it "go-slow". You can be on this bridge for two hours. People do their make-up in the car. They eat breakfast. They make business calls. They have whole relationships. I know a couple who met in go-slow. They got married.
ADA: But it's Saturday. So we're moving. Look at us. Flying. Well. Moving at the speed of a fast goat. But for Lagos on a bridge, that's flying.
ADA: The [[traffic congestion]] here is a big topic. The [[government]] has been building a new rail system, and there are new train lines now, which is great. But the city is growing faster than the roads and the trains can be built. The [[infrastructure]] is always trying to catch up.

# Pidgin Lesson
[bgm comedy.200 0.1]
ADA: While we're crossing, let me teach you some Pidgin. Because if you come to Lagos, you'll hear it everywhere.
ADA: Nigeria has more than five hundred languages. Five hundred! Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, and hundreds more. So how do people from different places talk to each other? English, officially. But on the street, in the market, in the danfo, it's Pidgin. It's a language that grew out of English, mixed with words from Nigerian languages, and it has its own grammar. Millions of people speak it every day. It's a real language, not "broken English". Don't let anybody tell you different.
ADA: Lesson one. "How far?" This means "How are you?" or "What's up?" And you answer, "I dey." Which means, "I'm fine. I'm here. I exist."
ADA: Lesson two. "Wetin dey happen?" That's "What's happening?"
ADA: Lesson three. "Abeg." That means "please". As in, "Abeg, shift small." Please, move over.
ADA: Lesson four, the most important one. "No wahala." No problem. Somebody steps on your foot in the market? No wahala. The danfo breaks down on the bridge? No wahala. NEPA takes light in the middle of your favourite film? Okay, that one's a bit of wahala. But mostly, no wahala.
ADA: And lesson five. "Na wa o!" This is what you say when something is shocking, or amazing, or too much. Like, "You waited three hours in go-slow? Na wa o!"
ADA: Okay. Try it with me. How far?
[pause 2.0]
ADA: Now answer me. I dey.
[pause 2.0]
ADA: Very good! You're practically a Lagosian now. Abeg, don't try to [[bargain]] with my aunty in Pidgin, though. She will destroy you.

# The Jollof War
[bgm sports.193 0.12]
ADA: Okay. For those of you who don't know, I need to explain the most serious [[conflict]] in West Africa.
ADA: Jollof rice. It's rice cooked in a sauce of tomatoes, peppers, onions, and spices, until it's red and rich and full of flavour. Every West African country makes it. And every country thinks theirs is the best. But the biggest rivalry, the one that never ends, is Nigeria against Ghana.
KOFI: There's no rivalry. Ghana's is better. That's not a rivalry. That's a fact.
ADA: You see? This argument has been going on for years. Online, mostly. There are memes. There are songs. There are cooking competitions on television. Families have stopped speaking. Okay, maybe not families. But Twitter, definitely.
KOFI: The difference is the rice. Ghanaians use jasmine rice, which is fragrant. Nigerians use long-grain parboiled rice, which is... fine. If you like fine.
ADA: Nigerian jollof is smokier. Especially party jollof. At a Nigerian party, they cook it in huge pots over a wood fire, and the rice at the bottom gets a little bit burned, and the smoke goes into everything. That smoky taste is the best taste in the world. People fight over the bottom of the pot.
KOFI: You're celebrating burned rice.
ADA: I'm celebrating flavour, Kofi!
KOFI: Okay, let's be serious for a moment. Honestly? The truth is, the jollof war is a love story. We only argue because we're so close. Same food, same music, same jokes. We're like brother and sister.
ADA: Aww. That's actually very sweet, Kofi.
KOFI: But Ghana's is still better.
ADA: And he's ruined it.

# Japa
[bgm piano.194 0.1]
ADA: Okay, while we walk to the car, I want to talk about something a bit more serious. Because a lot of you ask me about it in the comments.
ADA: There's a word that every young Nigerian knows right now. "Japa." It's Yoruba, and it means something like "to run away". But now, everybody uses it to mean leaving Nigeria to live abroad. To the UK, to Canada, to America, to anywhere.
ADA: In the last few years, a lot of young people have japa-ed. Doctors, nurses, engineers, programmers. The economy has been tough. Prices have gone up so fast. The [[cost of living]] is crazy. A bag of rice costs so much more than it did a few years ago. And when you're young and educated and you can't find a good job, or you can't [[afford]] a flat, and the power keeps going off, you start to think about leaving.
ADA: Half of my university friends are abroad now. My best friend is a nurse in Manchester. My cousin is in Toronto. We video call, and they show me their clean streets and their electricity that never goes off, and I'm happy for them. I really am.
ADA: People ask me, "Ada, why haven't you left?"
ADA: And honestly? Some days, I think about it. When I've been in go-slow for three hours, and NEPA has taken light again, and the generator is broken, believe me, I think about it.
ADA: But then I go to the market, and my aunty tries to sell me a fish shirt. And I eat suya on the street with my friends. And I hear the music. And I think, there's nowhere else on earth like this. Lagos is hard. But Lagos is alive. Everybody here is hustling, creating, building something. There's an energy here that you can't find anywhere else.
ADA: So for now, I'm staying. Maybe one day I'll japa too. But I hope, if I do, I'll come back. A lot of people do come back, and they bring new ideas with them. That's how a city grows.
