[sfx applause]

NICK: Hello, London! How are we all doing?
NICK (dry): That was a very British cheer, by the way. It was [[enthusiastic]], but not too enthusiastic. Like you were worried the neighbours might hear.
NICK: So, as you can probably tell from the accent, I'm not from round here. I'm from Australia.
NICK: Yes. Australia. The country where everything is trying to kill you. The spiders, the snakes, the sharks, the jellyfish, the sun. Even the plants. You're walking through the forest, minding your own business, you touch a leaf, and the leaf says, "No."
NICK: So I moved to London. For the safety. And the weather.
NICK (dry): That's a joke. Nobody has ever moved to London for the weather.

# The Weather
[sfx applause 0.4]
NICK: I've lived here for three years now. And the first thing I noticed about British people is that you love talking about the weather. You can't stop. It's like a [[disease]].
NICK: In Australia, we don't talk about the weather, because it's always the same. It's hot. It's sunny. "Lovely day." "Yep." That's it. That's the whole conversation. Then we go to the beach.
NICK: But here! Here, the weather is a soap opera. Every day there's a new episode. Every day there's a plot twist.
NICK (fast): You wake up, it's sunny. You go outside, it's raining. You go back in for an umbrella, it's sunny again. You leave the umbrella, it hails. Hail! In June! Little balls of ice falling out of the sky, in June, and everyone just carries on walking, like, "Oh, that's a bit much."
NICK: I asked a man at a bus stop once, "What's the [[forecast]] for tomorrow?" And he looked at me, very seriously, like a doctor about to give me bad news, and said, "Well. It could go either way."
NICK: Either way! What does that mean? There are only two ways? Wet and not wet?
NICK: And you've got so many words for it. In Australia, weather is either "hot" or "really hot". You lot have got... "drizzle", "showers", "spitting", "bucketing it down", "a bit grey", "close", "muggy", "fresh", "nippy", "brisk". My neighbour said to me the other day, "Bit fresh today, isn't it?" It was minus two. The car was frozen. A pigeon was stuck to a bench. "Bit fresh."
NICK: That's the thing about the British. You never exaggerate. You under-exaggerate. You take something [[enormous]] and make it small. A [[severe]] storm destroys half of Cornwall, and on the news there's a man standing in a flooded kitchen, water up to his knees, his sofa floating past, and he says, "Yeah, it's been a bit of a nuisance."
[sfx applause 0.4]

# The Secret Code
NICK: And that, ladies and gentlemen, brings me to the biggest problem I had when I moved here. Understanding you.
NICK: I had real [[culture shock]]. Because I thought you spoke English. And you do. Technically. The words are English. But the meanings? The meanings are in a secret code. And nobody gives you the code book when you arrive at Heathrow.
NICK: So I've made one. For all the other foreigners here tonight. I can see a few of you. Hello. Welcome. I'm sorry. Here's the code.
NICK (serious): Number one. "Not bad."
NICK: In Australia, "not bad" means "not bad". It's okay. It's fine. Seven out of ten.
NICK: In Britain, "not bad" means "this is the best thing that has ever happened to me". Your friend gets married to the love of her life, in a castle, with fireworks, and her dad says, "Well. That was not bad."
NICK (serious): Number two. "Quite good."
NICK: You'd think "quite good" is better than "not bad", right? Wrong! "Quite good" is a disaster! If a British person says your cooking is "quite good", throw it away. Throw the whole kitchen away. Leave the country.
NICK (serious): Number three. "I'll bear it in mind."
NICK: This means: "I have already forgotten what you said."
NICK (serious): Number four. "With the greatest respect..."
NICK: This means: "You are an idiot, and I'm about to explain why, slowly."
NICK (serious): Number five. "That's interesting."
NICK: This means: "That's wrong." Or: "That's terrible." Or sometimes: "I wasn't listening, but I can tell from your face that you've finished talking."
NICK (serious): And number six. The most dangerous one of all. "We should get a coffee sometime."
NICK: Oh, mate. Oh, I fell for this one so many times. When I first arrived, people would say, "Oh, Nick, we should get a coffee sometime!" And I'd get my phone out and say, "Great! When? Tuesday? I'm free on Tuesday!"
NICK: And they'd look at me with real fear in their eyes. Like I'd suggested we go swimming in the Thames.
NICK: Because, as I now know, "we should get a coffee sometime" means "I will never see you again, and if I see you in the street I'm going to hide behind a bin."
NICK: [[In other words]], the British are the only people in the world who can be extremely friendly and say absolutely nothing at the same time. It's a skill. I respect it. I can't do it. I'm Australian. If I don't like you, I just tell you. And then we go to the pub.
[sfx applause 0.5]

# Sorry
NICK: And then there's "sorry".
NICK: The British say "sorry" more than any other people on Earth. And I don't think you even notice.
NICK: You say "sorry" when it's your fault. Fine. Normal. You say "sorry" when it's not your fault. Okay. You say "sorry" when someone else bumps into you. Why? He bumped into you! He should be sorry! But no. You both say "sorry", at the same time, and then you both walk off feeling guilty for the rest of the day.
NICK: You say "sorry" instead of "excuse me". You say "sorry" instead of "pardon". You say "sorry" when you want to complain! "Sorry, but I think there's a fly in my soup." Why are you apologising? The fly should apologise!
NICK: But here's the scary part. It's [[contagious]]. After three years, I've caught it.
NICK: Last week, I was walking down Oxford Street, looking at my phone, and I walked straight into a lamppost. Bang. Right in the face. And do you know what I said? To the lamppost?
NICK (whisper): "Oh, sorry."
NICK: To a lamppost! I apologised to a piece of metal! My mother would be so ashamed.
[sfx applause 0.5]

# The Queue
NICK: Let's talk about queues.
NICK: Now, I know it's a [[stereotype]]. I'd heard about the British queue before I came. Everybody has. It's famous. But nothing prepares you for seeing it in real life.
NICK: In Australia, we have a sort of queue. It's more of a... suggestion. A loose crowd, all [[roughly]] facing the same direction. If someone pushes in, we say, "Oi! Back of the line, mate!" And they say, "Fair enough." And everyone's happy.
NICK: Here, a queue is a [[religion]]. Queue [[etiquette]] is more important than the law. It's perfect. It's silent. It's straight. You could use a British bus queue to check if a wall is level.
NICK: And you queue for things that don't need a queue. I once saw a queue at a bus stop where there was only one person. He was standing in a very neat line. By himself. He was queuing behind... nobody. Just in case someone came and tried to stand in front of him.
NICK: And if anybody pushes in, here's what happens. Nothing. Absolutely nothing. Nobody says anything. But you can feel it. The whole queue goes cold. Everybody stares at the back of that person's head with pure hate. Twenty people, silently, all thinking the same sentence: "Unbelievable."
NICK: Then they go home and they tell their families about it. For years. "Did I ever tell you about the man who pushed in at the post office in two thousand and nine?" "Yes, Grandad. Every Christmas."
NICK: That's British revenge. You don't fight. You don't shout. You just remember. Forever.
[sfx applause 0.5]

# Slang
NICK: And then, once you've learned the code, you have to learn the slang. And British slang is a whole other language.
NICK: In my first week at work, a guy came into the office on a Monday morning and said, "I'm absolutely knackered." And I didn't know what that meant, so I just said, "Oh, congratulations!"
NICK: He was tired. "Knackered" means tired. Very tired. I congratulated a man for being [[exhausted]]. He looked at me like I was a lunatic, which, to be fair, I was.
NICK: Then the next day, the same guy says, "I'm gutted." And I thought, gutted? That's what you do to a fish! Are you okay? Do you need a doctor? No. "Gutted" means very [[disappointed]]. His football team had lost.
NICK: The day after that, his team won, and he said, "I'm chuffed." Chuffed means happy. So in one week, this man went from knackered, to gutted, to chuffed. And I learned more English from him than from my entire time at school.
NICK: But the best one, the one that confused me the most, was when I went to buy some lunch, and the woman in the sandwich shop said, "There you go, love. Cheers, ta, ta-ra."
NICK: Cheers, ta, ta-ra! That's three words! And none of them are real! "Cheers" means thank you. "Ta" means thank you. And "ta-ra" means goodbye. So she said "Thank you, thank you, goodbye", using three words that I had never heard in my life, in one second, while handing me a sandwich.
NICK: And you know what I said back? I'd been here a week. I was trying so hard. I panicked. And I said:
NICK (nervous): "Ta... cheers... to you... also."
NICK: She just nodded, very kindly, the way you'd nod at a dog that's trying to tell you something.
NICK: Now I can do it. Three years later. Watch. "Cheers, ta, ta-ra." Nailed it. Took me three years. That's one word a year. At this rate, I'll be [[fluent]] in British English when I'm about ninety.
[sfx applause 0.5]

# Tea
NICK: Of course, I can't do a show in London without talking about tea.
NICK: Now, I like tea. Australians drink tea. But we drink it like normal people. When we're thirsty.
NICK: The British don't drink tea because they're thirsty. You drink tea for every single human emotion. Happy? Cup of tea. Sad? Cup of tea. Someone died? Cup of tea. Someone was born? Cup of tea. The house is on fire? "Let's just have a cup of tea and think about this calmly."
NICK: In my first month here, I made tea for my new colleagues at work. To be friendly. To fit in. And I did something terrible. I didn't know. Nobody told me. I put the milk in first.
[pause 0.8]
NICK: You see? Some of you just gasped. There's a woman in the front row who's gone pale. Are you alright, madam? Do you need a cup of tea?
NICK: The office went completely silent. Like I'd insulted the King. One man, Graham, from accounts, took the cup, looked into it for a very long time, and then said, very quietly...
NICK (dry): "That's an interesting way to make it."
NICK: And now you all know the code! "Interesting" means "terrible"! Graham hated me! Graham hated me for two years, and he never said a word!
NICK: We're friends now. He came to my birthday. He brought his own tea bags.
[sfx applause 0.5]

# Mum Comes to Visit
[bgm jazz.62 0.08]
NICK: Last summer, my mum came to visit me from Sydney. Now, my mum is very Australian. She's sixty-five, she's very [[outgoing]], and she has never, ever had a thought that she didn't say out loud.
NICK: So, picture this. My mum, in London, in a room full of very polite British people. It was like watching a kangaroo walk into a library.
NICK: The first night, I took her to a dinner party at my friend's flat in Clapham. Lovely people. Very posh. Candles everywhere. And my friend's wife had cooked this very fancy dish, some kind of fish with foam on it. And she said, "Oh, it's nothing special, really. I just threw it together." Which, as you all know, in the code, means "I've been cooking for nine hours and I'm going to cry if you don't like it."
NICK: And my mum, who does not know the code, tasted it, put her fork down, and said...
NICK (loud): "Ooh, that's a bit salty, isn't it, love?"
NICK: Silence. Absolute silence. Someone dropped a spoon. I think a candle went out by itself.
NICK: And my friend's wife smiled. The big British smile. The dangerous one. And she said, "Oh, do you think so? How interesting."
NICK: And I'm sitting there, sweating, because I know the code now. I know exactly what "interesting" means. I know we're never going to be invited back. Ever.
NICK: And my mum, totally happy, says, "No worries! I'll have some more anyway!" And she did. Three plates. And told everyone the story of my birth, in detail, with hand movements.
NICK: Mum, if you're listening, I love you. Please never come back.
[bgm off]
[sfx applause 0.5]

# Tokyo
NICK: Now. I thought the British were the most polite people in the world. And then, last year, I went to Japan.
NICK: Oh. Oh, my friends. I had no idea. The British are [[amateurs|amateur]]. You're beginners. Japan is the [[Olympic Games]] of politeness.
NICK: In Japan, when you walk into a shop, all the staff shout at you. All of them, together, really loud: "Irasshaimase!" It means "Welcome!" But I didn't know that. The first time it happened, I thought I'd done something wrong. I thought I was being arrested. I put my hands up. In a convenience store. Holding a rice ball.
NICK: The cashier just bowed at me and gave me my change with two hands. Two hands! With a little smile. For a rice ball that cost about a pound. In London, if you're lucky, the man in the shop looks at you. Once. Without love.
NICK: And then there's the bowing. In Japan, people bow to say hello, to say thank you, to say sorry, to say goodbye. And I wanted to be respectful, so I bowed back. Every time. To everyone.
NICK: And this is where it went wrong. Because I didn't know the rules. I didn't know when to stop.
NICK: On my last day, I was checking out of my hotel, and the manager came out to say goodbye. Lovely man. Grey suit. And he bowed. So I bowed. And then he bowed again, a bit lower, to thank me for bowing. So I bowed again, lower, to thank him for thanking me. And then he bowed again.
NICK (fast): And we just kept going! Bow! Bow! Bow! Lower and lower! It was like two pigeons fighting over a chip! Neither of us could stop, because stopping would be rude!
NICK: I missed my train. I genuinely missed my train to the airport. I had to get a taxi. That bow cost me eighty pounds.
NICK: And you know what? It was worth it. Best eighty pounds I ever spent. That man and I shared something special. I think about him every day. I think he's still bowing.
NICK: But here's my theory. If you ever put a very polite British person and a very polite Japanese person in a room together, and there's one biscuit left on the plate...
NICK (dry): Nobody will ever eat that biscuit. They'll both say "No, please, you have it", for the rest of time. Archaeologists will find them in a thousand years. Two skeletons. One biscuit.
[sfx applause 0.5]

# Home

NICK: And, yeah. Of course I do. I miss the sun. I miss the beach. I miss the sky, that big blue sky that goes on forever. I miss people saying exactly what they mean. I miss my mum. Even though she insulted a fish.
NICK: But I'll tell you what. I've started to love this place. I love that you're so polite, even when you're furious. I love that you say "cheers" instead of "thank you", and "cheers" instead of "goodbye", and sometimes "cheers" instead of everything. I love that a whole country can stop and have a cup of tea in the middle of a crisis.
NICK: And I love that when something truly amazing happens, something that would make an Australian scream and jump into the sea, you just nod, very slowly, and say...

NICK (dry): "Not bad."

NICK: So, London. You've been a wonderful [[audience]]. I can say that, because I'm Australian, so I actually mean it.
