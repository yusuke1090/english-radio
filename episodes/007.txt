# The Café in Manchester
PRIYA: Thank you. Thank you so much.
PRIYA: I want to start with a cup of coffee.
PRIYA: Eleven years ago, I moved from Chennai, in the south of India, to Manchester, in the north of England, to do my PhD. I had been speaking English since I was four years old. I had read Shakespeare. I had read Dickens. I had a [[degree]] in English [[literature]]. I thought, I'm ready.
PRIYA: On my first morning, I walked into a café near the university, and I asked for a coffee. And the young man behind the counter looked at me, smiled very kindly, and said:
PRIYA (fast): "Alright, love, what can I get you, d'you want owt with that?"
[pause 1.0]
PRIYA: I stood there. I didn't understand one single word.
PRIYA: And it wasn't the words, really. "Owt" is a northern English word for "anything", but I didn't know that yet. It was the music of it. The rhythm, the vowels, the speed. It was English, I knew it was English, but it was an English I had never heard before.
PRIYA: And then, the worst part. I answered him. And he didn't understand me either.
PRIYA: He said, "Sorry?" I said it again. He said, "Sorry, love?" I said it again, slower, louder, the way you speak to someone who's very old, or very far away. And he went red, and I went red, and in the end I pointed at a picture of a croissant, and I ate a croissant I didn't want, and I went home and I cried.
PRIYA: Because in that moment, a voice in my head said something that I think many of you in this room have heard too. It said: "Your English isn't good enough. It's your accent. It's wrong."
PRIYA: I spent the next ten years [[finding out|find out]] that this voice was wrong. And tonight, I want to tell you why.

# Everybody Has One
[bgm piano.51 0.1]
PRIYA: Let's start with something simple. Put your hand up if you have an accent.
PRIYA: Okay. About half of you. Interesting. Now put your hand up if you have a mouth.
PRIYA: Everybody. Good. Then you all have an accent.
PRIYA: This is the first thing that linguists, people who study language scientifically, will tell you. There is no such thing as speaking without an accent. An accent is just the way your speech sounds. It's the shape of your vowels, the rhythm of your sentences, the way your voice goes up and down. Every single person who speaks, speaks with an accent.
PRIYA: The queen of England had an accent. A newsreader on the BBC has an accent. A cowboy in Texas has an accent. And the young man in the café in Manchester definitely had an accent. Nobody had ever told him that it was a problem.
PRIYA: So why do some accents feel like "normal English", and other accents feel like "mistakes"?
PRIYA: The answer has almost nothing to do with language. It has to do with power. With history. With which accents we hear on television, in films, in the voices of people in charge. The accents that we're used to hearing from powerful people start to sound "correct" to us. And the others start to sound "strange", or "foreign", or "difficult".
PRIYA: But listen. This is just habit. It's not science. A Scottish accent is not a broken English accent. An Indian accent is not a broken American accent. A Nigerian accent, a Japanese accent, a Brazilian accent, are not broken anything. They're just different. They're the sound of different histories.
PRIYA: Here's a fact that surprises a lot of people. Today, there are far more people in the world who speak English as a second language than as a first language. Some [[estimates|estimate]] say it's around three to one. Think about that. If you speak English with an accent from somewhere else, you're not the [[exception]]. You're the [[majority]].
PRIYA: The typical English speaker in the world today is not someone from London or New York. It's someone from Lagos, or Manila, or Mumbai, or Shanghai, talking to someone from São Paulo, or Istanbul, or Tokyo. And they're using English as a bridge.
[bgm off]

# Englishes
[bgm piano.58 0.1]
PRIYA: And when English travels, it doesn't just change its accent. It changes its words. It makes new ones. It [[borrows|borrow]] from the languages around it. Linguists have a word for this. They don't talk about "English" any more. They talk about "Englishes". Plural.
PRIYA: Let me give you a few of my favourites.
PRIYA: In India, where I'm from, we have a word that I think the whole world needs. "Prepone." You know "postpone", when you move a meeting to later? Well, if you move it earlier, in India, you "prepone" it. It's [[logical]]. It's useful. And every time I use it in England, someone corrects me. They're wrong. English needs this word.
PRIYA: In South Africa, if someone says they'll do something "now", it means... at some point. If they say "just now", it means later. Maybe in an hour. Maybe tomorrow. But if they say "now now", it means soon. Really soon. I have a South African colleague, and it took me two years to [[figure out]] when he was actually going to send me his emails.
PRIYA: In Nigeria, if you phone a friend and they say "I'm coming", they don't mean they're on their way to you. They mean "wait a moment, I'll be right back". A British friend of mine waited outside a restaurant in Lagos for forty minutes, because his friend said "I'm coming" and then went to the toilet.
PRIYA: In Singapore, you might hear "can" used as a complete answer. "Can you meet at six?" "Can." "Can lah." It's short, it's [[efficient]], and it's absolutely clear.
PRIYA: In Ireland, if everything is fine, it's "grand". If everything is a disaster, it's also "grand", but said [[slightly]] differently.
PRIYA: In Australia, the afternoon is the "arvo". Breakfast is "brekkie". A barbecue is a "barbie". Australians look at a long word and think, "No. Too long. Cut it."
PRIYA: Now. Are these mistakes? Are these people speaking "bad English"?
PRIYA: Of course not. These are perfectly good, fully grammatical varieties of English, used by millions of people every day. They follow rules. They're [[consistent]]. They do exactly what language is supposed to do. They let people understand each other.
PRIYA: So when someone tells you there is only one "correct" English, ask them, politely: whose?

# The Research
[bgm piano.52 0.1]
PRIYA: Now, I'm a scientist, so I don't want you to just believe me. Let me tell you about some research.
PRIYA: In the nineteen nineties, two researchers in Canada, Murray Munro and Tracey Derwing, did some very simple but very important experiments. They recorded people who were learning English, speaking short sentences. Then they asked native English speakers to listen, and to judge three different things.
PRIYA: First: how strong is this person's accent? Second: how much of what they said did you actually understand? You can check this, by asking listeners to write down exactly what they heard. And third: how hard did you have to work to understand them?
PRIYA: And what they found, again and again, in study after study, was this. Those three things are not the same.
PRIYA: A person can have a very strong accent, and still be completely understood. Every single word. Their accent is strong, but their speech is clear.
PRIYA: I want you to really think about that, because it's very different from what most of us were taught at school. We were taught that the goal is to sound like a native speaker. To lose our accent. To hide where we come from.
PRIYA: But the research says the goal isn't to lose your accent. The goal is to be understood. And those are two very different goals. One is almost impossible for most adults. The other is completely achievable.
PRIYA: So let me give you some new words. Instead of "accent", let's talk about "intelligibility". It's a long word. It just means: can people understand you? And "comprehensibility": how easy is it for them?
PRIYA: Those are the things that matter. Not whether you sound like you're from London.
[bgm off]

# What Actually Matters
[bgm piano.53 0.1]
PRIYA: So if it's not about sounding "native", what does make speech easy to understand?
PRIYA: After years of research, and years of teaching, I've come to believe that a few things matter much more than others. And interestingly, they're not usually the sounds that teachers spend the most time on.
PRIYA: Many of us spent hours at school practising the "th" sound. Th, th, think, this, that. And it's fine. Practise it if you like. But here's the thing. In many parts of the English-speaking world, native speakers don't use it either. In parts of London, "think" sounds like "fink". In Ireland, "three" can sound like "tree". And everybody understands them perfectly, because the listener knows from the [[context]] what they mean.
PRIYA: What matters more, much more, is stress and rhythm. Which part of a word is strong, and which words in a sentence are strong.
PRIYA: Let me show you. Listen. "Photograph." "Photographer." "Photographic." Can you hear it? The strong part of the word moves. And if you put the stress in the wrong place, a listener can get completely lost, even if every single sound is perfect.
PRIYA: There's a well-known example that teachers love. A speaker says the word "comfortable", but puts the stress in the middle, on "for". And the listener hears... "come for a table". Same sounds. Different music. Completely different meaning.
PRIYA: The same is true of sentences. English is a language with a strong beat. We hit the important words, and we rush through the small ones. "I want to go to the shop." Not "I. Want. To. Go. To. The. Shop." If every word is equally strong, the listener doesn't know where to look. It's like a page where every single word is underlined.
PRIYA: So if you're learning English and you want to be understood, here's my advice. Spend less time on making perfect sounds, and more time on the music. Listen to the rhythm. Copy it. Exaggerate it. Sing it, even. You'll be [[amazed]] how much clearer you become, even with exactly the same accent.
[bgm off]

# Try It
[bgm piano.59 0.08]
PRIYA: Let's try it. Right now. Yes, everybody. Even you at the back, pretending to check your phone. And if you're listening to this in your car, you can join in too. Nobody can hear you. Probably.
PRIYA: I'll say a sentence, and you say it back to me. Don't worry about the sounds. Just copy the beat. The strong words are like steps. The small words are what happens between the steps.
PRIYA (slow): I need a cup of tea.
[pause 2.5]
PRIYA: Good. Again, and this time, make the small words really small. Almost nothing. "I need a cup of tea." "A cup of" becomes "a-cuppa".
PRIYA (slow): I need a cup of tea.
[pause 2.5]
PRIYA: Lovely. Now a longer one.
PRIYA (slow): I was going to call you, but I forgot.
[pause 3.0]
PRIYA: One more time. Listen to how "was going to" becomes almost one word. "I was gonna call you."
PRIYA (slow): I was going to call you, but I forgot.
[pause 3.0]
PRIYA: And one more. This is the most useful sentence in the English language.
PRIYA (slow): Sorry, could you say that again?
[pause 3.0]
PRIYA: Beautiful. You see? Every one of you said that with your own accent. Spanish, Korean, Scottish, Chinese, whatever. And every one of you was completely clear. Because you got the music right.
PRIYA: And notice something else. The last sentence, "Sorry, could you say that again?", is not a sign of failure. It's a sign of a good conversation. The best communicators in the world use it all the time. They check. They ask. They don't pretend.

# The Other Half of the Conversation
[bgm piano.54 0.1]
PRIYA: Now. I have to say something that might make some people in this room a bit uncomfortable.
PRIYA: Communication is not a one-way street. If two people don't understand each other, it is not automatically the fault of the person with the "foreign" accent.
PRIYA: Listening is a skill too. And like any skill, it can be trained.
PRIYA: Remember the young man in the café in Manchester? It [[turns out|turn out]] that the more different accents you hear, the better you get at understanding new ones. Researchers have shown that even a short period of exposure to an unfamiliar accent can make it much easier to understand. Your brain is brilliant at this. It learns the patterns very fast, if you give it the chance.
PRIYA: So if you are a native speaker of English, and you work with people from other countries, or you're a teacher, or a doctor, or a shopkeeper, here's my request. Do your half of the work. Listen with patience. Don't pretend you've understood when you haven't. Don't shout. Shouting doesn't help anybody understand anything. And don't [[assume]] that someone with an accent is less intelligent, or less educated, than you.
PRIYA: Because here's the sad truth. Studies have found that people with non-native accents are sometimes judged as less [[trustworthy]], or less competent, even when they say exactly the same thing. Not because of what they say, but because of how it sounds. That's not a language problem. That's a [[prejudice]].
PRIYA: And in job interviews, in hospitals, in [[courts|court]], that prejudice has real [[consequences|consequence]] for real people. It decides who gets the job. It decides who gets believed.
PRIYA: So when we talk about accents, we're not just talking about vowels. We're talking about fairness.
[bgm off]

# My Grandmother's Voice
[bgm piano.55 0.1]
PRIYA: I want to tell you about my grandmother.
PRIYA: My grandmother, my paati, as we say in Tamil, grew up in a small village in Tamil Nadu. She left school at twelve. She spoke Tamil, and a little Hindi, and when she was forty years old, she decided to learn English, because she wanted to be able to read the newspaper and talk to the doctors in the city.
PRIYA: She learned from the radio. From old textbooks. From her grandchildren, who were not very patient teachers. And she spoke English for the rest of her life with a very, very strong Tamil accent. Every "w" became a "v". Every sentence went up and down like a song.
PRIYA: And when I was a teenager, I'm ashamed to say this, I was [[embarrassed]] by it. When my friends came to the house, I didn't want her to speak English in front of them. I thought her English was... wrong.
PRIYA: She died when I was twenty-two. And a few years later, I found some old cassette tapes in a box. My grandfather had recorded her reading to us, when we were small. Children's stories, in English. In that accent.
PRIYA: I listened to those tapes, sitting on the floor of my flat in Manchester, and I understood something I should have understood years before.
PRIYA: Her accent wasn't a mistake. It was a map. Every sound in it was a place she had come from. The village. The temple. The radio. The forty-year-old woman who sat down at the kitchen table and decided that it wasn't too late.
PRIYA: Every accent is like that. When you hear someone speaking English with an accent, you are hearing their journey. You're hearing the other languages they speak, the places they've lived, the effort they've made. You're hearing a person who has done something very difficult, every single day, and is still doing it.
PRIYA: That's not something to hide. That's something to be proud of.
[bgm off]

# Back to the Café
[bgm piano.56 0.1]
PRIYA: So. Let's go back to the café in Manchester.
PRIYA: About a year after that first terrible morning, I went back to the same café. The same young man was working there. His name, it turned out, was Danny. And this time, I'd been in Manchester long enough. I knew what "owt" meant. I knew that "love" didn't mean he loved me. I knew that "you alright?" didn't mean "are you sick?", it just meant "hello".
PRIYA: And Danny, I think, had heard a lot more accents in that year, because the university was full of international students, and half his [[customers|customer]] were from China, or Nigeria, or Brazil.
PRIYA: So I walked in, and he said, "Alright, love, what can I get you?" And I said, "A flat white, please, and nothing with it, thank you." In my accent. And he understood me perfectly, first time.
PRIYA: Neither of us had changed our accent. Not one bit. We had both just... learned to listen.
PRIYA: We became good friends, actually. He still makes my coffee. He still says "owt". And last year, he started learning Tamil, because he's going out with a girl from Chennai.
PRIYA: His accent is terrible.
PRIYA: I've never been prouder of anyone.
[bgm off]

# The Last Thing
[bgm piano.57 0.1]
PRIYA: So I want to leave you with three small things.
PRIYA: If you're learning English, or any language: don't try to delete yourself. Don't waste years trying to sound like somebody else. Work on being clear. Work on the rhythm. Be understood. And then, keep your accent. It's yours. You earned it.
PRIYA: If you're a native speaker: do your half. Listen generously. Ask again, kindly, if you don't understand. And the next time you hear someone speaking with an accent you don't know, don't think "that's wrong". Think "where has that voice been?"
PRIYA: And for all of us: remember that the point of language was never to sound the same. The point of language was always to understand each other.
PRIYA: And that is something we can all get better at. Every single one of us. In every accent there is.
PRIYA: Thank you.
