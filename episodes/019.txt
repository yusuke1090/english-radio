# The Night Shift
[bgm lofi.204 0.13]
LEO: "City Lights at One A.M." I love that one. It sounds like driving through a city when all the traffic lights are green, just for you.
LEO: You know, we don't think about it much, but a huge part of the world keeps going all night. Somebody is always awake. Nurses and doctors in hospitals. Police officers. Firefighters. Bakers, making tomorrow's bread. Cleaners, in empty offices. [[Security]] guards. Taxi drivers. Lorry drivers, bringing food to the supermarkets before the doors open. Factory workers. Call centres, answering phones for people on the other side of the world, where it's daytime.
LEO: In a lot of countries, something like one in five workers does some kind of [[shift]] work. Early mornings, evenings, nights. And night work is hard on the body. Humans aren't [[nocturnal]] animals. We're built to be awake in the light and asleep in the dark.
LEO: Inside each of us, there's a kind of clock. A twenty-four-hour rhythm that tells the body when to feel sleepy, when to feel hungry, when to feel alert. Light is what sets that clock. That's not just a theory, it's been shown in [[experiments|experiment]] again and again. So when you work all night and try to sleep in the day, you're fighting your own biology. It causes a lot of [[stress]] for the body. People who work nights for many years have a higher risk of all kinds of health problems. Sleep problems, stomach problems, heart problems. It affects your [[well-being]], and your [[mental health]] too.
LEO: So if you're one of those people working tonight, I just want to say, from me, thank you. The world works because you're awake. We don't say that enough.

# Three A.M. Thoughts
[bgm space.207 0.12]
LEO: "Empty Platform". A little piano for a quiet moment.
LEO: Can I talk about something? Something I think a lot of us know, but don't talk about much?
LEO: Three a.m. thoughts.
LEO: You know the ones. You wake up in the middle of the night, for no reason, and suddenly your brain decides it's the perfect time to think about everything. That embarrassing thing you said at a party in two thousand and twelve. Whether you locked the door. Money. Work. Your health. Whether you're wasting your life. And in the dark, at three in the morning, every problem looks ten times bigger than it is.
LEO: Here's the strange thing. In the morning, when the sun comes up, most of those thoughts just... shrink. You have a coffee, and you look at the problem again, and it's normal size. Annoying, maybe. But manageable.
LEO: I read once that there's something real behind this. At night, when you're tired, the parts of your brain that keep your emotions in balance don't work quite as well. So your worries get louder, and your calm, sensible voice gets quieter. It's not that your life is worse at three a.m. It's that your brain is too tired to see it clearly.
LEO: So, if you're lying awake right now, with your mind going round and round... here's my advice. And I'm not a doctor. I'm a guy with a microphone. But. Don't trust your three a.m. thoughts. Write them down, if you need to. Then tell them, very politely, that you'll [[deal with]] them in the morning. Most of them won't even turn up.

# Light Pollution
[bgm space.212 0.12]
LEO: "Last Bus to Nowhere". A little bossa nova for the long road.
LEO: Mike's talk about the stars made me think of something. When was the last time you saw the Milky Way? I mean really saw it. That big, cloudy river of light across the sky.
LEO: For most of human history, everybody could see it, every clear night. It was just there. It's in the stories and the myths of almost every [[culture]] on Earth. But today, about [[a third]] of all the people on Earth can't see it from where they live. In Europe and North America, it's most people. Not because it's gone. Because of us.
LEO: It's called [[light pollution]]. All the street lights, and car parks, and buildings, and [[advertising]] signs, send light up into the sky, and it bounces around in the air, and it creates a sort of glow over every city. And that glow is brighter than the faint stars. It washes them out.
LEO: Scientists have studied this. They've found that the night sky has been getting brighter every year, as cities grow, and as more lights go up. In a big city today, you might see a few dozen stars, at most. In a truly dark place, far from any town, you can see thousands.
LEO: And it's not just about the view. A lot of animals depend on the dark. Birds that [[migrate]] at night use the stars to find their way, and city lights can confuse them. Baby sea turtles, when they hatch on the beach, head towards the brightest light, which used to be the moon over the sea. Now, sometimes, it's a hotel.
LEO: The good news is that this is one kind of pollution that's easy to fix. Turn lights off when you don't need them. Point them down, not up. Some towns are doing exactly that, and some places have even become official "dark sky" areas, where the night is protected. And people travel there, just to see the stars, the way their great-grandparents saw them every night.
LEO: So, a challenge for you. Sometime this year, go somewhere dark. Really dark. Lie on your back. Wait twenty minutes for your eyes to adjust. And look up. I promise, it'll change something in you.
LEO: Here's a track for the city at night, with all its lights on. It's called "Convenience Store at Three A.M.", and it's inspired by a friend of mine in Tokyo, who says the most peaceful place in the world is a Japanese convenience store at three in the morning. Bright and clean and quiet, with soft music playing, and one sleepy guy behind the counter, and rice balls.
