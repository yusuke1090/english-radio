SAM: Okay, so, this is the not-so-fun side of farming. I'm going to keep filming, but I'll explain as I go.
SAM: Milk fever is something that can happen to a cow a day or two after she's given birth. She suddenly starts making a huge amount of milk, and milk is full of calcium. So all the calcium in her blood goes into the milk, faster than her body can [[replace]] it. And without enough calcium, her muscles stop working properly. She gets wobbly, and then she lies down, and she can't get up again.
SAM: If you don't treat it quickly, it's very serious. She could die. But the good news is, if you catch it early, the treatment is simple, and it works fast. Like, really fast. It's honestly one of the most amazing things you'll ever see.
SAM: Here we are. Okay, there she is. Number four one two. She's lying down, her head's turned round to the side. Classic milk fever. Joel's already got the bag ready.
JOEL: Calcium's here.
SAM: So we give her calcium, straight into the vein, slowly. Slowly is really important. Too fast, and it can be dangerous for her heart. So we just... wait. And keep talking to her.

# Afternoon
[bgm lofi.31 0.13]
SAM: Okay, it's the afternoon now, and I wish I could tell you that I had a nice relaxing lunch. I had a sandwich, standing up, in the tractor.
SAM: After lunch, I fed out some [[silage]]. That's grass that we cut in the summer and store in a big pile under plastic. It's like pickled grass. The cows love it, even though it smells like vinegar and socks.
SAM: Then I spent an hour moving fences. We use electric tape to divide the paddocks into strips, so the cows only get a fresh strip of grass each time. It means the grass gets eaten evenly, and then it has time to grow back. If you let them into the whole paddock at once, they eat the best bits and walk all over the rest.
SAM: Then at half past two, guess what? We do it all again. Afternoon milking. Four hundred and twenty cows. Karen kicked the cups off again. Of course she did.
