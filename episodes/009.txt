# Food and Culture
RYAN: Good morning, and welcome to English Radio. I'm Ryan.
SONIA: And I'm Sonia. Thanks for joining us again on your way to work.
RYAN: Today, we're going to talk about food. But not about recipes or restaurants. We're going to talk about why people eat what they eat, and especially, how [[religion]] shapes our food.
SONIA: It's a fascinating subject, because food is often much more than fuel. It's connected to identity, family, and [[belief]]. Our guest is Neerja, a food writer from Mumbai, in India. Neerja, welcome to English Radio.
NEERJA: Thank you so much, Sonia and Ryan. It's lovely to be with you.
RYAN: Neerja, India seems like the perfect place to start. Why is that?
NEERJA: Because India is home to so many religions. The [[majority]] of people are Hindu, but there are also large Muslim, Christian and Sikh communities, as well as Buddhists, Jains and others. Each of these traditions has its own ideas about food. So in one Indian city, you can find an incredible range of food customs, sometimes in the same street, or even in the same office canteen.
SONIA: Let's start with Hinduism. Many people associate India with [[vegetarian]] food. Is that accurate?
NEERJA: Partly. India has more vegetarians than any other country. In one large survey, around four in ten Indian adults described themselves as vegetarian. But that also means most Indians do eat meat, at least sometimes. It really depends on the region, the community and the family.
RYAN: And the cow is sacred in Hinduism, isn't it?
NEERJA: Yes. For many Hindus, the cow is a symbol of motherhood and generosity, because she gives milk, which nourishes people. So most Hindus don't eat beef, even those who eat chicken or fish. In many Indian states, it's actually illegal to [[slaughter]] cows.
SONIA: So the cow is protected, but her milk is very important.
NEERJA: Extremely important. Dairy is everywhere in Indian food. Ghee, which is clarified butter, is used in cooking and in religious ceremonies. Paneer, a fresh cheese, is a favourite for vegetarians. And then there's yoghurt, lassi, sweets made from milk, and of course chai.
RYAN: Of course chai. This show can't escape tea.
NEERJA: Nobody in India can escape tea, either! And here's something your listeners might find interesting, after your episode on Kenya. India is the largest milk producer in the world. And a big part of that success came from dairy cooperatives.
SONIA: Just like Wanjiru's cooperative in Kenya.
NEERJA: Very similar. In the nineteen seventies, India started a huge programme to organise small dairy farmers into cooperatives. Millions of farmers with just one or two buffaloes or cows could sell their milk together, get fair prices, and reach the cities. People call it the White Revolution. It changed the lives of millions of rural families.
RYAN: That's a great connection. Now, what about other religions in India?
NEERJA: Let's take Jainism, which is a smaller religion but very interesting when it comes to food. The central idea of Jainism is non-violence towards all living beings. So Jains are strictly vegetarian, and many of them go further. They avoid root vegetables, like onions, garlic, potatoes and carrots.
SONIA: Why root vegetables?
NEERJA: Because when you pull a root vegetable out of the ground, you kill the whole plant, and you may also harm many tiny creatures living in the soil. So a lot of Jain cooking is done without onion or garlic, which is quite a challenge for a cook. Some very devout Jains also don't eat after sunset.
RYAN: That's an [[ethical]] position taken very seriously.
NEERJA: It is. And it's not just about rules. For many people, food is a daily way of practising their values.
SONIA: What about Islam?
NEERJA: Muslims eat food that is halal, which means permitted. Pork is not allowed, and neither is alcohol. Meat has to come from animals slaughtered in a specific way, with a prayer. And of course, there's Ramadan, the holy month when Muslims fast from dawn until sunset every day. No food, and not even water, during daylight hours.
RYAN: Not even water? That must be very hard, especially in hot weather.
NEERJA: It is very hard. But the evening meal, called iftar, when people break their fast, is a beautiful time. Families and neighbours eat together, and traditionally the fast is broken with dates and water. In Mumbai, during Ramadan, some streets fill with food stalls in the evening, and people of all religions come to enjoy the food.
SONIA: That sounds wonderful. And the Sikh community has a famous food tradition too, doesn't it?
NEERJA: Yes, the langar. This is my favourite example of food and faith. Every Sikh temple, which is called a gurdwara, has a free community kitchen. Anyone can come in and eat a simple vegetarian meal, whatever their religion, background or social class. Everyone sits together on the floor, side by side, to show that all people are equal.
RYAN: Anyone at all?
NEERJA: Anyone at all. At the Golden Temple in Amritsar, the most important Sikh temple, [[volunteers|volunteer]] cook and serve free meals for tens of thousands of people every day. It's one of the largest free kitchens in the world. I volunteered there once, making chapatis, and it was an unforgettable experience.
SONIA: That's a truly [[generous]] tradition. And there are langars in Britain too, aren't there?
NEERJA: Yes, in many British cities with Sikh communities. During the pandemic, many gurdwaras in Britain delivered free meals to people who were [[isolated]] or struggling.
RYAN: Let's widen the picture. What about religions beyond India?
NEERJA: Every religion has its food customs. In Judaism, people who keep kosher don't mix meat and [[dairy products]] in the same meal. In Christianity, some people give up certain foods during Lent, the weeks before Easter. In Britain, the tradition of eating fish on Fridays, and the popularity of fish and chips on a Friday, comes partly from Christian customs.
SONIA: I didn't know that. Fish and chips Friday has a religious history!
NEERJA: Many everyday habits do. And in Japan, there's shojin ryori, the vegetarian cooking of Buddhist temples. It's beautiful food, very seasonal, made with tofu, vegetables, seaweed and mountain plants, and no meat or fish at all.
RYAN: I've heard that some temples in Kyoto serve it to visitors.
NEERJA: Yes, and I highly recommend it. It's a very peaceful way to eat.
RYAN: Neerja, many religious festivals have special foods too, don't they?
NEERJA: Oh, absolutely. Festivals are when food becomes really exciting. During Diwali, the Hindu festival of lights, families make and share huge amounts of sweets, which we call mithai. People visit each other's homes with boxes of sweets, and by the end of the festival, everyone has eaten far too much sugar.
SONIA: That sounds a bit like Christmas in Britain.
NEERJA: Very similar! And at Eid, the festival at the end of Ramadan, Muslim families prepare big feasts. Biryani, which is a fragrant rice dish with meat, and a sweet milky dessert made with thin noodles, dates and nuts.
RYAN: That sounds delicious.
NEERJA: It is. And in the south of India, there's a harvest festival called Pongal. The name comes from a dish of rice cooked in milk with jaggery, which is a kind of unrefined sugar. Families cook it outdoors in a new clay pot, and they let it boil over on purpose.
SONIA: On purpose? Why?
NEERJA: Because the milk overflowing from the pot is a symbol of abundance, of having more than enough. When it boils over, everyone shouts pongalo pongal! It's a wonderful moment.
RYAN: I love that. In my kitchen, when milk boils over, it's a disaster. In India, it's a [[celebration]].
NEERJA: Exactly! It's all about how you look at it. And Christmas is celebrated in India too, especially in states like Kerala and Goa, where there are large Christian communities. People make rich fruit cakes, a bit like a British Christmas cake.
SONIA: That's a lovely example of [[cultural exchange]].
NEERJA: Yes. And in a big city like Mumbai, people from different religions often celebrate each other's festivals together. My Hindu neighbours bring us sweets at Diwali, and my Muslim friends invite everyone for food at Eid. Food is how we share our festivals.
SONIA: Neerja, for people who travel, or who invite guests from different backgrounds, what advice would you give?
NEERJA: The most important thing is simply to ask. If you're inviting someone to dinner, ask, is there anything you don't eat? It's polite, and nobody will be offended by the question. And when you travel, learn a few basic customs. In India, for example, people traditionally eat with their right hand, and many restaurants are labelled pure veg, which means they serve no meat or eggs at all.
RYAN: That's useful to know. Are there any signs on food packaging too?
NEERJA: Yes! In India, packaged food has a small symbol: a green dot in a square means it's vegetarian, and a brown or red one means it's not. It makes shopping much easier for vegetarians. I wish every country had it.
SONIA: That's a really simple and clever system. Neerja, thank you so much for this.
NEERJA: Thank you both. And remember, sharing food is the oldest way of making friends.

# The Report
SONIA: Time now for The Report. Ryan, I think you're keeping the food theme going.
RYAN: I am, Sonia. In two thousand and one, the British Foreign Secretary, Robin Cook, gave a speech about British [[identity]]. In it, he said that chicken tikka masala was now a true British national dish. It was a surprising statement, but many people agreed.
RYAN: Chicken tikka masala is pieces of grilled, spiced chicken in a creamy, orange-coloured tomato sauce. It's one of the most popular dishes in Britain. But where does it come from? Its origins are disputed. One of the most popular stories says that it was invented in the nineteen seventies in a restaurant in Glasgow, in Scotland, when a customer complained that his chicken tikka was too dry, and the chef created a sauce to go with it. Others say similar dishes existed in India long before. Nobody can prove it either way.
RYAN: What is certain is that Britain's love of curry has a long history. The first Indian restaurant in London opened in eighteen ten, more than two hundred years ago. But the real boom came after the Second World War, when many [[immigrants|immigrant]] arrived from South Asia. Many of the curry houses that opened across Britain from the nineteen sixties onwards were started by families from Bangladesh, especially from the region of Sylhet.
RYAN: These restaurants [[adapted|adapt]] their cooking to British tastes. Dishes became milder, or much hotter, than in South Asia, and new dishes were created especially for British customers. In this way, the British curry house developed its own style, related to, but different from, the food of India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.
RYAN: Going out for a curry became a British tradition, especially on a Friday night. For many towns, the local curry house became as familiar as the pub or the fish and chip shop. Robin Cook's point in his speech was that chicken tikka masala showed how Britain absorbs and adapts outside influences, making a [[multicultural]] society.
RYAN: Today, however, many curry houses are facing difficult times. Rising costs for energy and ingredients have put pressure on small restaurants. Owners also report staff shortages, as stricter [[immigration]] rules have made it harder to bring in experienced chefs. And many of the founders' children and grandchildren, who grew up in Britain, have chosen other careers, such as medicine, law or business, rather than taking over the family restaurant.
RYAN: At the same time, the way Britain eats South Asian food is changing. A new generation of restaurants focuses on regional dishes, home-style cooking, and street food from cities like Mumbai and Kolkata. Many diners now want a more [[authentic]] experience, and supermarkets sell a huge range of spices and ready-made sauces for cooking at home.
RYAN: Whatever the future holds, the story of curry in Britain is a story of migration, hard work and adaptation. It shows how food can become a bridge between cultures, and how what counts as national [[cuisine]] can change over time. That's the report for today.
SONIA: Thanks, Ryan. Now I really want a curry.
RYAN: It's eight o'clock in the morning, Sonia.
SONIA: A curry is always a good idea.

# Daily Talk
RYAN: Now, Sonia, for our chat today, I want to ask you a question about your working habits.
SONIA: Go on.
RYAN: Do you listen to music while you work?
SONIA: It depends what I'm doing. If I'm answering emails or doing something simple, then yes, I have music on. But if I need to read something carefully, or write something important, I need silence.
RYAN: Interesting. I'm the opposite. I can't [[concentrate]] in silence. It's too quiet. I start noticing every tiny sound, like someone eating crisps three desks away.
SONIA: The crisp eater. Every office has one.
RYAN: Every office has one. So I put on headphones and listen to music without words. Usually something calm, like piano music, or those long videos of relaxing beats.
SONIA: Lo-fi music?
RYAN: Exactly. Very popular with students too. There's a famous livestream that plays it all day and all night, with a cartoon of a girl studying.
SONIA: I think the key thing for me is the lyrics. If a song has words, especially in English, my brain starts listening to them, and I lose track of what I'm reading.
RYAN: I think a lot of people find that. When you're reading or writing, your brain is already busy with language, so words in the music compete with the words on the page.
SONIA: That makes sense. And what about the famous Mozart effect? I remember people saying that listening to Mozart makes you more intelligent.
RYAN: Ah, that's a bit of a myth. It started with a small study in the nineteen nineties, where people did slightly better on one type of puzzle after listening to Mozart for a few minutes. But the effect was small and short. Then the idea got exaggerated, and people started playing Mozart to babies to make them geniuses.
SONIA: So it doesn't make babies smarter?
RYAN: There's no good [[evidence]] that it does. But listening to music you enjoy can put you in a better mood, and a better mood can help you work. So in a way, any music you like can have a small Mozart effect.
SONIA: I like that. What about in the car? What do you listen to when you drive?
RYAN: When I drive, which isn't often these days, I like something with energy. Rock music, usually.
SONIA: I've read that fast music can make people drive faster.
RYAN: Some studies have suggested that, yes. So maybe calm music is safer in heavy traffic.
SONIA: Or a nice, calm English radio show.

RYAN: There's actually a strange cure that one study found. Chewing gum.
