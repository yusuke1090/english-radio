# Farm and Tech
SONIA: Good morning, and welcome to English Radio. I'm Sonia.
RYAN: And I'm Ryan. Thanks for spending your drive with us again.
SONIA: So far in this series, we've visited farms in New Zealand, Kenya, Australia, Ireland and Canada. Big fields, lots of grass, lots of cows.
RYAN: Today's farm is very different. There are no fields, no cows, and the farm might be on top of a car park.
SONIA: We're going to Singapore, a small island city in Southeast Asia, with around six million people and very little land. Our guest is Wayne, who runs an urban farm there. Wayne, welcome to the show.
WAYNE: Thank you, Sonia. Good morning, or rather, good afternoon from Singapore.
RYAN: Wayne, let's start with the problem. Why does Singapore need urban farms?
WAYNE: Because we have almost no farmland. Singapore is a city, a country and an island all at once, and every piece of land is needed for housing, offices, roads, schools and parks. Less than one per cent of our land is used for farming. So we [[import]] more than ninety per cent of our food from other countries.
SONIA: Ninety per cent! That must make people a bit nervous.
WAYNE: It does. Our [[food security]] depends on other countries and on global supply chains. Normally, that works well. Singapore is rich and well connected, and we buy food from all over the world. But when something goes wrong, we feel it immediately.
RYAN: Can you give an example?
WAYNE: During the pandemic, many people suddenly realised how fragile supply chains are. And a couple of years later, our neighbour Malaysia temporarily stopped exporting chicken, because of shortages at home. That was a shock for Singapore, because one of our favourite dishes is chicken rice. People were genuinely worried about where their chicken rice would come from.
SONIA: The national dish in danger.
WAYNE: Exactly. So the government set an ambitious goal to produce much more of our own food locally. It turned out to be harder than expected, and the targets have been adjusted since then. But the idea is still the same: produce more at home, and buy from many different countries, so we don't depend too much on any single one.
RYAN: So where do you actually grow food in a city with no land?
WAYNE: Anywhere we can! One of the most interesting places is the roofs of multi-storey car parks. In Singapore, most people live in large public housing blocks, and next to them there are car parks with flat roofs. The government started renting out these roofs to farmers. So you can drive up to the top floor, and there's a vegetable farm.
SONIA: That's brilliant. Using space that was completely wasted.
WAYNE: Exactly. And then there are vertical farms, which is what we do.
RYAN: What exactly is a vertical farm?
WAYNE: Instead of growing plants side by side on the ground, you stack them in layers, one above another, like shelves in a supermarket. Our farm is inside a building, and the shelves go up about ten levels. There's no sunlight. Instead, each layer has LED lights, which give the plants exactly the light they need.
SONIA: What colour are the lights?
WAYNE: Often a pinkish purple, because plants mainly use red and blue light. When visitors first walk in, they say it looks like a nightclub for lettuce.
RYAN: A nightclub for lettuce! And what about soil? Do you use soil?
WAYNE: No soil at all. We use hydroponics. The plant roots hang in water that contains all the nutrients they need. The water flows round and round in a closed system, so very little is wasted. Compared with a normal field, we use a tiny fraction of the water.
SONIA: That sounds very efficient. What else are the advantages?
WAYNE: Many. Because we're indoors, there's no bad weather, no floods, no droughts, and almost no pests, so we don't need to spray [[pesticides|pesticide]]. We can grow all year round, and we can harvest in the morning and deliver to supermarkets the same day, because the farm is in the middle of the city. Very fresh, and very few food miles.
RYAN: It sounds almost too good to be true. What's the catch?
WAYNE: The catch is energy. In a field, the sun gives you light for free. In a vertical farm, you pay for every single photon. Lights, air conditioning, pumps, all of that needs electricity, and electricity is expensive. Energy is our biggest cost.
SONIA: And I suppose that also affects the environment.
WAYNE: It does. If the electricity comes from fossil fuels, the [[carbon footprint]] of the vegetables can be quite high. That's why we're working hard on [[energy efficiency]]: better LEDs, better insulation, and some [[solar panels|solar panel]] on the roof. But I'll be honest, the energy question is the biggest challenge for our whole industry.
RYAN: I've read that several big vertical farming companies in Europe and America got into serious financial trouble a few years ago.
WAYNE: Yes, that's true. A few years ago, there was a lot of excitement, and [[investors|investor]] put huge amounts of money into vertical farming. Some [[start-ups|start-up]] grew too fast, built enormous farms, and then energy prices went up sharply. Several of them went [[bankrupt]]. It was a painful lesson for everyone.
SONIA: So is vertical farming actually [[viable]], as a business?
WAYNE: For the right crops, in the right places, yes. Leafy greens like lettuce, spinach and pak choi work well, because they grow quickly, they're light, and people pay for freshness. Herbs and microgreens work too. Some companies grow strawberries indoors very successfully. But for staple crops like rice or wheat, it makes no sense. They need too much space and too much light, and the price per kilo is low. A loaf of bread made from vertical-farm wheat would be ridiculously expensive.
RYAN: So we're not going to see rice growing on the fortieth floor.
WAYNE: Not any time soon. Rice will keep coming from the fields of Thailand, Vietnam and elsewhere. Vertical farms are a complement to traditional farming, not a replacement.
SONIA: You mentioned rice. I know Japan has been doing this kind of thing for a long time.
WAYNE: Yes, Japan is a real pioneer. People there call them plant factories, and Japan has been building them since the nineteen eighties. We've learned a lot from Japanese engineers and researchers. Some Japanese plant factories grow lettuce very efficiently, with robots doing much of the work.
RYAN: Are there other ways Singapore is trying to produce food?
WAYNE: Yes, lots. There are high-tech egg farms, and fish farms out at sea. And Singapore became the first country in the world to approve the sale of cultivated meat, back in twenty twenty. That's real meat grown from animal cells in a factory, without raising and slaughtering an animal.
SONIA: Meat grown from cells. That sounds like science fiction.
WAYNE: It does, and it's still very small and quite expensive. But it shows that Singapore is willing to experiment. When you have so little land, you have to be [[innovative]].
SONIA: Wayne, before we talk about your day, I have to ask about food culture in Singapore. I've heard the hawker centres are incredible.
WAYNE: Ah, the hawker centres. They're the heart of Singapore. A hawker centre is a big open-air food court, with dozens of small stalls, each selling one or two dishes. Chicken rice, laksa, which is a spicy noodle soup, satay, roti prata, and much more. Chinese, Malay, Indian and Peranakan food, all in one place.
RYAN: That sounds like the perfect lunch.
WAYNE: It is. And it's cheap. You can have a delicious meal for just a few dollars. People from every background eat there side by side, office workers in suits next to grandmothers and students.
SONIA: Like the yatai in Japan, or the night market in Zanzibar.
WAYNE: Very similar. Street food brings everyone together. In fact, Singapore's hawker culture was added to UNESCO's list of Intangible cultural heritage a few years ago.
RYAN: That's impressive. A food court as world heritage.
WAYNE: And a few hawker stalls have even received a Michelin star. The first one sold soy sauce chicken with rice, for only a few dollars a plate. The queue became so long that people waited for hours.
SONIA: Michelin-star food for a few dollars. That's amazing value.
WAYNE: It is. But the hawkers themselves are facing a challenge. Many of them are getting old, and their children don't want to take over. The work is very hard. Long hours, hot kitchens, and low prices. So people worry that some traditional dishes may disappear.
RYAN: That's the same story we heard about Japanese farmers and British curry houses.
WAYNE: It's a global story. The older generation has the skills, but young people want different careers. In Singapore, there are now programmes where young people can learn directly from experienced hawkers, a bit like an [[apprenticeship]].
SONIA: That's a lovely idea. Passing the recipe from one generation to the next.
RYAN: Wayne, what's a typical day like at your farm?
WAYNE: Very clean! Before entering the growing area, we wear special clothes, hairnets and shoe covers, a bit like a hospital, to keep out pests and bacteria. Then we check the data. Every shelf has sensors for temperature, humidity, carbon dioxide and the nutrients in the water. If something changes, my phone tells me.
SONIA: Just like the cow collars and the soil sensors we heard about earlier in the series.
WAYNE: Farming everywhere is becoming more about data. Then we have seeding, harvesting and packing. A lot of it is still done by hand, but we're adding more [[automation]] every year.
RYAN: And do you enjoy it?
WAYNE: I love it. My grandparents were farmers in Malaysia, with chickens and vegetables. When I tell my grandmother I'm a farmer too, she laughs, because she can't believe you can farm without soil or sun. But when she tasted our lettuce, she said it was good. That was the best review I've ever had.
SONIA: That's lovely. Wayne, thank you so much.
WAYNE: Thank you both. If you're ever in Singapore, come and visit the lettuce nightclub.

# The Report
RYAN: Now it's time for The Report. Sonia, I believe this one's about something green.
SONIA: That's right. Walk into a café in London, New York or Sydney today, and you'll probably see it on the menu: matcha. Matcha lattes, matcha ice cream, matcha cakes, even matcha doughnuts. In just a few years, this bright green Japanese tea has gone from a speciality to a global [[trend]].
SONIA: Matcha has a long history. Powdered green tea was brought from China to Japan in the twelfth century by Buddhist monks, and it later became the centre of the Japanese tea ceremony, a ritual of preparing and serving tea with great care and attention.
SONIA: What makes matcha different from ordinary green tea is the way it is grown and made. For several weeks before harvest, the tea plants are covered to block out most of the sunlight. This makes the leaves a deeper green and gives them a richer, sweeter, more savoury taste. After harvest, the leaves are steamed, dried, and their stems and veins are removed. Then they are slowly ground into a fine powder, traditionally between heavy stones. A single stone mill can produce only a small amount of powder per hour.
SONIA: Demand has grown rapidly in recent years. Social media has played a big part. The bright green colour looks good in photos, and matcha has a healthy image, partly because it contains caffeine together with a substance that many people say produces a calm, focused feeling. Japan's green tea exports have reached record levels.
SONIA: But [[supply]] has struggled to keep up. Producers cannot simply make more overnight. New tea plants take several years to grow before they can be harvested, and covering the fields and processing the leaves requires special equipment and skilled workers. Like many parts of Japanese agriculture, the tea industry is also facing an ageing workforce, with fewer young people taking over family farms.
SONIA: As a result, there have been [[shortages|shortage]]. Some tea shops in Kyoto have limited how much matcha each customer can buy, and prices have risen sharply. Some farmers have switched from producing other teas to producing leaves for matcha, to [[meet the demand]].
SONIA: There is also some confusion for [[consumers|consumer]]. Matcha sold abroad is often labelled ceremonial grade or culinary grade, but these are mainly marketing terms rather than official Japanese standards. Experts advise buyers to look at the colour, the origin, and how fresh the tea is.
SONIA: For Japanese tea farmers, the boom is both an opportunity and a challenge. Higher prices are welcome after years of falling demand for green tea at home. But some worry that the boom may not last, and that investing heavily in new equipment could be risky if fashions change. For now, though, the world's appetite for this ancient green powder shows no sign of slowing down. That's the report for today.
RYAN: Thanks, Sonia. I have to admit, I've never tried matcha.
SONIA: Never? Not even a matcha latte?
RYAN: Not even a matcha latte. I'm a simple man. Tea with milk.
SONIA: Then that's your homework for this week.

# Daily Talk
