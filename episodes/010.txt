# Evening
[amb night 0.5]
[music afro.82 14]
[amb fire 0.9]
[bgm afro.83 0.1]
FEMI (warm): Come, come. Sit down. Closer to the fire. Tolu, move up, let your cousin sit. There's room for everybody.
FEMI: Good. Is everybody comfortable? Has everybody eaten? Good. Because a story on an empty stomach is like rain on a tin roof. Very loud, and nobody remembers it.
TOLU: Uncle Femi, are you going to tell the spider story?
FEMI: Maybe. Maybe. First, let me tell you how we begin.
FEMI: When I was a small boy, in my grandmother's village, near Abeokuta, in the south-west of Nigeria, there was no television. There was no phone. When the sun went down, it was dark, really dark, and the only light was the fire. So in the evenings, all the children would sit around my grandmother, just like you're sitting around me now. And she would tell us stories.
FEMI: My grandmother spoke Yoruba. And in Yoruba, when a storyteller wants to begin, she calls out, "Alo o!" And all the children shout back, "Alo!" That means: we are ready. Tell us.
FEMI: So. Let's try it. Alo o!
TOLU (excited): Alo!
FEMI: That was very weak. I've heard louder goats. Alo o!
TOLU (shout): Alo!
FEMI (happy): Good! Now we can begin.
FEMI: These [[folk]] stories are very old. Some of them are [[ancient]]. Nobody wrote them down for hundreds of years. They lived only in people's mouths, and people's memories. They were passed down from [[generation]] to generation. Grandmothers told them to grandchildren, who grew up and told them to their own grandchildren. Every storyteller changed them a little bit. So if you hear these stories somewhere else, they might be a bit different. That's fine. That's how a story stays alive. A story that never changes is a dead story.
FEMI: This is our [[cultural heritage]]. It doesn't live in a [[museum]]. It lives right here, around this fire. And now you're going to carry it. So listen carefully.

# Story One: How Anansi Bought the Stories
[music afro.84 8]
[bgm afro.85 0.1]
FEMI: The first story isn't from Nigeria. It comes from our neighbours in Ghana, from the Ashanti people. And it's about the most famous character in all of West Africa.
TOLU: The spider!
FEMI: The spider. Kwaku Anansi. Anansi is small. Anansi is weak. Anansi is lazy, and greedy, and he tells lies. But Anansi is also the cleverest creature in the whole forest. He can't win a fight, so he wins with his head.
FEMI: And the Anansi stories travelled a very long way. Hundreds of years ago, when people from West Africa were taken across the [[ocean]], to the Caribbean and to the Americas, they lost almost everything. Their homes, their families, their names. But they kept their stories. So today, you can hear stories about Anansi in Jamaica, in the Caribbean [[islands|island]], and in America. The little spider crossed the ocean with them.
FEMI: Now. A long, long time ago, at the beginning of the world, there were no stories on Earth. Not one. People worked, and ate, and slept, and that was all. In the evenings, they just sat in silence and looked at each other. Imagine that. No stories at all.
TOLU: That's so boring.
FEMI: Extremely boring. Because all the stories in the world belonged to one person. Nyame, the Sky God. He kept them in a big wooden box, up in the sky, and he didn't share them with anybody.
FEMI: Anansi wanted those stories. So he spun a long, long thread of silk, all the way up to the sky, and he climbed it, and he went to see Nyame.
FEMI (serious): "Great Sky God," he said, "I would like to buy your stories."
FEMI: Nyame laughed so hard that thunder rolled across the sky.
[sfx thunder 0.5]
FEMI: "You? A tiny spider? Kings have come to me to buy these stories. Rich men from great cities have come with gold. And all of them went home with nothing. What can you give me?"
FEMI (serious): "Tell me the price," said Anansi.
FEMI: So Nyame thought of the most impossible price he could. "Bring me four things. Bring me Onini, the python, the greatest [[predator]] in the forest, who can swallow a goat. Bring me Osebo, the leopard, with teeth like knives. Bring me Mmoboro, the hornets, who sting like fire. And bring me Mmoatia, the fairy whom nobody ever sees. Bring me all four, and the stories are yours."
FEMI: Anansi bowed. He was [[determined]]. And he climbed back down to Earth. And he went home, and sat down, and thought. And thought. And thought.
TOLU: How can a spider catch a python?
FEMI: That is exactly what Anansi asked himself. And this is what he [[came up with|come up with]].
FEMI: First, the python. Anansi cut a long, straight branch from a palm tree. Then he walked through the forest, very near to where Onini the python lived, arguing loudly with himself.
FEMI (fast): "He's longer than this branch! No, he's not! Yes, he is! No, he's definitely not! I'm telling you, he's much shorter!"
FEMI: The python heard this, and he came out of the grass. "Anansi, what are you shouting about?"
FEMI: "Oh, Onini! Thank goodness. My wife and I are having an argument. She says you're longer than this branch. I say you're much shorter. We can't agree."
FEMI: Now, the python was very proud of his length. "Shorter? Ridiculous! Put the branch down, and I'll show you."
FEMI: So the python lay down along the branch. "But, Onini," said Anansi, "when you stretch at one end, you get shorter at the other. Let me tie your head to the branch, just to be fair. There. And your tail. There. And your middle. There."
FEMI: And when the python was tied up tight, from head to tail, Anansi said, "Well, it looks like my wife was right. You are longer. Now, come with me. Somebody in the sky would like to meet you."
TOLU (happy): Ha! He tricked him!
FEMI: He tricked him. That's one.
FEMI: Next, the leopard. Anansi dug a deep hole in the ground, right on the path where Osebo the leopard walked every night. He covered the hole with leaves and branches. And in the night, the leopard walked along, and... whoosh! Down he fell, into the hole.
FEMI: In the morning, Anansi came and looked down. "Osebo! My poor friend! What happened? Here, let me help you out. I'll bend down this tree, and tie it to your tail, and pull you up."
FEMI: The leopard was so [[grateful]]. But as soon as his tail was tied, Anansi let go of the tree, and it sprang back up, and the leopard was hanging upside down by his tail, too high to bite anybody. That's two.
FEMI: The hornets were next. Anansi filled a dried gourd, a kind of hollow fruit, with water. He walked to the hornets' nest, poured some water over the nest, and poured the rest over his own head. Then he held a big leaf over his head like an umbrella and shouted, "Hornets! Hornets! It's raining! Your nest will be destroyed! Quickly, fly into this gourd, it's dry in here!"
FEMI: And the hornets, thinking they were being rescued, flew straight into the gourd. And Anansi put a lid on it. That's three.
FEMI: And the last one, the fairy, Mmoatia. Nobody ever sees her. So how do you catch someone you can't see?
TOLU: I don't know.
FEMI: Anansi carved a little wooden doll. He covered it in sticky gum from a tree, so sticky that anything that touched it would never come off. He put the doll at the foot of a tree where the fairies played. And in front of the doll, he put a bowl of the best yams you have ever tasted.
FEMI: Mmoatia came. She ate all the yams. Then she said to the doll, "Thank you for the yams." The doll said nothing. "I said thank you," said the fairy. The doll said nothing. "How rude!" said the fairy. "Answer me, or I'll slap you!" Still nothing. So she slapped the doll. And her hand stuck. So she slapped it with the other hand. And that stuck too. Then she kicked it, and her foot stuck, and then the other foot, and then she was completely stuck to the doll, and Anansi came out from behind the tree, smiling. That's four.
[bgm off]
[bgm afro.86 0.1]
FEMI: So Anansi climbed back up his silk thread to the sky, with a python, a leopard, a gourd full of hornets, and a very angry fairy. And he put them all down in front of Nyame.
FEMI: And all the gods and all the elders in the sky were silent. Nobody had ever done this. Kings had tried, and failed. And a tiny spider had done it.
FEMI: And Nyame stood up, and said, "From today, all the stories in the world belong to Anansi. We will call them Anansi stories."
FEMI: And Anansi carried the big wooden box down to Earth, and opened it. And the stories flew out, all over the world, into every village and every house. And people never sat in silence in the evenings again.
FEMI: And that is why, to this day, in many places, all stories, even stories that have nothing to do with spiders, are called Anansi stories.
TOLU: So this story is an Anansi story too?
FEMI: Every story is an Anansi story, my child. Even yours.
[bgm off]

# Questions
[bgm acoustic.87 0.1]
TOLU: Uncle Femi, but Anansi lied to everybody. He's a bad person. Why is he the hero?
FEMI: Ah. That's a very good question. That's the question a [[curious]] child asks.
FEMI: Think about it like this. Anansi is small. All the animals he tricked were bigger and stronger than him. The python, the leopard, the hornets. If he fought them, he'd lose. Every time. So he uses the only thing he has. His brain.
FEMI: For hundreds of years, the people who told these stories were often poor, and often not powerful. Farmers. Ordinary people. People who had kings and chiefs and rich men above them. And they loved a hero who was small, like them, but smarter than the big, strong, important people. When Anansi tricks the leopard, the farmer listening by the fire laughs, because, just for a moment, the little one wins.
FEMI: But Anansi isn't always the winner. In lots of stories, he's too clever for his own good. He's greedy, or [[selfish]], or [[arrogant]], and in the end, he gets into trouble. So you can laugh at him and learn from him at the same time. He's a hero and a warning, both together.
TOLU: Like my brother.
FEMI: Exactly like your brother. Don't tell him I said that.
FEMI: Now. Let's have another story. Alo o!
TOLU (shout): Alo!
[bgm off]

# Story Two: Why the Sun and the Moon Live in the Sky
[music space.88 8]
[bgm space.89 0.1]
FEMI: This story comes from the Efik people, in the south-east of Nigeria, near the sea. And it's a story about why things are the way they are. Why the Sun and the Moon are up in the sky, and not down here with us.
FEMI: A long time ago, the Sun and the Moon lived on the Earth. They were husband and wife. They had a nice house, with a garden, and lots of friends.
FEMI: And the Sun's best friend in the whole world was Water. Every day, the Sun would go to visit Water, and they would sit together and talk and laugh for hours.
FEMI: But Water never came to visit the Sun. Never. Not once.
FEMI: One day, the Sun said, "My friend, I always come to your house. Why do you never come to mine?"
FEMI: And Water said, "I'm sorry, my friend. I'd love to come. But my family is very, very big. My people are many. If I came to your house, we'd fill it completely. We'd push you out of your own home."
FEMI: The Sun laughed. "Don't be silly! I'll build a bigger house. A huge house. Big enough for everybody. Then you'll come?"
FEMI: Water said, "Then I'll come."
FEMI: So the Sun went home and told his wife, the Moon. And together, they built a new house. An [[enormous]] compound. With high walls, and many, many rooms. It took them months. It was the biggest house in the whole world.
FEMI: And when it was finished, they invited Water to visit.
FEMI: Water came to the gate, and called out, "My friend! Can my people and I come in?"
FEMI: "Of course! Come in, come in! You're welcome!"
FEMI: So Water began to flow in through the gate. And with Water came the fish. And the crabs. And the shrimps. And the frogs, and the crocodiles, and the water snakes, and the turtles, and all the creatures of the river and the sea.
[amb off]
[amb waves 0.7]
FEMI: Soon, the water was up to their knees. Water called out, "Is it safe for my people to keep coming in?"
FEMI: And the Sun, who was a good host, and didn't want to be rude, said, "Yes, yes, come in!"
FEMI: Soon, the water was up to their waists. "Shall we keep coming?" "Yes! Come!"
FEMI: Soon, the water was up to their necks. The Sun and the Moon climbed up onto the table. "Should my people keep coming?" said Water.
FEMI: And the Sun, who had promised, and who was too polite to change his mind, said, "Yes. Come."
FEMI: So the water rose higher, and higher, until it was right up to the roof. And the Sun and the Moon climbed up onto the top of the roof. And still the water came, and still it rose. Until finally, the Sun and the Moon had to jump. Up, up, off the top of the roof, and into the sky.
FEMI (calm): And they've lived there ever since.
[amb off]
[amb fire 0.9]
TOLU: But why didn't the Sun just say "Stop"?
FEMI: Because he'd made a promise. And because he was polite. Sometimes, being too polite can cost you your house.
FEMI: But I like to think the Sun is happy up there. He can see the whole world. And every day, he looks down at his old friend Water, and he shines on him, and makes him sparkle. And the Water looks up, and reflects him back. They're still friends. They just live in different places now.
TOLU: Like Auntie Bisi in London.
FEMI: Just like Auntie Bisi in London. She phones every Sunday. And she sparkles.
[bgm off]

# Story Three: The Pot of Wisdom
[music afro.90 8]
[bgm afro.91 0.1]
FEMI: One more story. The last one. And then bed.
TOLU (sad): Noooo.
FEMI: Yes. But it's a good one. It's Anansi again. Alo o!
TOLU (shout): Alo!
FEMI: Once, a long time ago, Anansi had an idea. He thought, "I am the cleverest creature in the world. But I could be even cleverer. What if I had all the wisdom in the world? All of it. Then I would be the wisest, and everybody would have to come to me for advice. And pay me."
FEMI: So Anansi took a big clay pot, and he went from house to house, from village to village, all over the world. And from every person he met, he took a little bit of their wisdom and put it in the pot. The farmer's wisdom about when to plant. The fisherman's wisdom about the tides. The grandmother's wisdom about children. The doctor's wisdom about medicine. The chief's wisdom about people. Everything that anybody knew, Anansi collected.
FEMI: Until finally, the pot was full. All the wisdom in the world was in one pot. And Anansi held it in his arms, very carefully.
FEMI: Now, he had a problem. Where could he hide it? If he kept it in his house, someone might steal it. So he decided to hide it at the top of the tallest tree in the forest. A great silk-cotton tree, taller than anything else, where nobody could ever reach it.
FEMI: So he tied the pot to the front of his body, on his stomach, and he started to climb.
FEMI: But it was impossible. The pot was big and round, and it was in the way. Every time he reached up, the pot bumped against the tree. He slipped. He fell. He tried again. He slipped. He fell again. He was getting very angry and very tired.
FEMI: And down at the bottom of the tree, his young son, Ntikuma, was watching.
FEMI (cheerful): "Father," said the little boy. "Why don't you tie the pot on your back? Then you could [[hold on]] to the tree, and climb easily."
[pause 1.0]
FEMI: Anansi stopped. He looked down at his son. He looked at the pot. He realised that his little boy was right.
FEMI: And he realised something much worse. He had all the wisdom in the world, in a pot, tied to his own stomach. And he still hadn't thought of that simple idea. A small child had.
FEMI: Anansi was so angry, and so [[embarrassed]], that he threw the pot down onto the ground.
[sfx slam 0.5]
FEMI: And the pot broke into a thousand pieces. And all the wisdom flew out, into the wind. The wind carried it everywhere. Over the forest, across the rivers, over the mountains and the seas. And a little bit of wisdom landed on every single person in the world.
FEMI (calm): And that, children, is why nobody has all the wisdom. Everybody has a little. The farmer, the fisherman, the grandmother, the child. Even you. Even me.
[bgm off]

# The Last Word
[bgm acoustic.92 0.1]
TOLU: Uncle Femi, what does it mean?
FEMI: What do you think it means?
TOLU: Um... that you shouldn't be greedy?
FEMI: Good. What else?
TOLU: That... small children can be clever too?
FEMI (happy): Very good! I like that one. What else?
TOLU: That... you should listen to other people? Even if they're younger than you?
FEMI: Yes. That's the one I like best. The wisest person is not the one who knows everything. Nobody knows everything. Even the spider couldn't carry it all up one tree. The wisest person is the one who knows that everybody else knows something too. And who listens.
FEMI: My grandmother used to say that. She couldn't read or write. But she knew a hundred stories, and she knew every plant in the forest, and she knew exactly what every child was thinking, before they knew it themselves. She was the wisest person I ever knew.
FEMI: And she gave her stories to me. And tonight, I gave them to you.
FEMI (warm): So now you have to promise me something. One day, when you're old, and you have grandchildren of your own, you will sit them down by a fire, and you will say...
TOLU (shout): Alo o!
FEMI (happy): And they will say...
TOLU (shout): Alo!
FEMI (warm): That's it. That's how stories live forever. They're [[passed on|pass on]], one fire at a time.
FEMI: Now. Bed. All of you. Go on. And if you hear a little spider in the corner of your room tonight... be nice to it. You never know. It might be Anansi, looking for a new story.
