# City Pop
[bgm lofi.8 0.14]
MAYA: Imagine Japan in the early nineteen eighties. The economy is [[booming|boom]]. It's a time of incredible [[economic growth]]. Young people in Tokyo have money in their pockets for the first time. They've got new cars, and in those cars, they've got brand-new stereo systems. And a few years earlier, Sony had released a little cassette player that you could carry in your pocket. The Walkman.
MAYA: And the music that filled those cars, and those Walkmans, was something people now call "city pop".
RAJ: [[Back in the day]], my uncle had a whole box of those tapes.
MAYA: Of course he did. City pop is bright, smooth, a little bit jazzy, a little bit funky. It sounds like neon lights on a wet street at night. It sounds like driving along the coast in a convertible, even if you're actually on a bus.
MAYA: For years, almost nobody outside Japan knew about it. And then, around twenty seventeen, something strange happened. A song from nineteen eighty-four, "Plastic Love", by the singer Mariya Takeuchi, started appearing on YouTube recommendations. All over the world. Millions of people who had never heard of her, and couldn't understand the Japanese lyrics, fell in love with it.
MAYA: It [[went viral|go viral]], nearly forty years after it came out. Then other songs followed. Suddenly teenagers in Brazil and Poland and Kenya were listening to Tokyo pop from before they were born.
RAJ: That's the internet for you. Nothing ever really dies.
MAYA: I love that. People feel [[nostalgic]] for a time and a place they've never even been to. So, this first track is our tribute to that sound. It's called "Neon Rain on Route 246". Turn it up.

# Bossa Nova
[bgm bossa.9 0.14]
MAYA: Ah, that bass line. "Neon Rain on Route 246". If you're stuck in traffic right now, I hope that made it feel a bit more like a music video.
RAJ: It did not. I'm in a studio with no windows.
MAYA: Raj has no windows, everyone. Send him your sympathy.
MAYA: Right, from Tokyo we're flying west, a long way west, to Rio de Janeiro, in Brazil. The late nineteen fifties.
MAYA: In the apartments near Copacabana beach, a group of young musicians were getting together at night and playing quietly. Very quietly, partly because the neighbours complained. And out of those quiet nights came a new style of music. They called it "bossa nova". In Portuguese, that means something like "the new trend", or "the new way".
MAYA: The heart of bossa nova is the guitar. A singer and guitarist called João Gilberto developed a way of playing that sounds simple, but is incredibly hard to copy. The thumb plays a soft bass note, and the fingers play the chords in a rhythm that sort of... floats. It's samba, slowed down and whispered.
RAJ: It's music for people who don't want to wake up the baby.
MAYA: Honestly, that's not a bad description. And in the nineteen sixties, bossa nova travelled all over the world. "The Girl from Ipanema" became one of the most recorded songs in history. It was a fantastic example of [[cultural exchange]]. American jazz musicians fell in love with Brazilian rhythms, and Brazilian musicians had grown up listening to American jazz. Each one had a huge [[influence]] on the other.
MAYA: So, the second song tonight is a gentle one. Perfect for the moment when the traffic finally starts moving again. It's called "Saudade in the Slow Lane". "Saudade" is a beautiful Portuguese word with no perfect translation. It means a deep, sweet sadness, a longing for something you love that isn't here.
RAJ: Like my lunch.
MAYA: Like Raj's lunch. Here it is.

# Highlife
[bgm afro.11 0.14]
MAYA: Our next stop is West Africa. And to understand this one, we need to go back about a hundred years, to the coast of Ghana.
MAYA: In the early twentieth century, in the port cities, local musicians started mixing traditional Ghanaian rhythms with the instruments that sailors and soldiers brought from Europe and the Caribbean. Guitars, trumpets, saxophones. And the result was a style called "highlife".
MAYA: There's a story about the name. People say it was called highlife because it was played at fancy parties and clubs, where the rich people went. The "high life". Ordinary people would stand outside the fence and listen.
RAJ: The original free concert.
MAYA: Exactly. In the nineteen fifties, a bandleader called E. T. Mensah became so famous that people called him the King of Highlife. And highlife spread to Nigeria, and across the whole region.
MAYA: Then, in the nineteen seventies, in Lagos, a musician called Fela Kuti took highlife, mixed it with American funk and jazz, made the songs much longer, and much more political, and created something he called Afrobeat. Some of his songs are more than twenty minutes long.
RAJ: That's a whole commute. One song.
MAYA: One song, the whole way home. And today, the grandchildren of all that music are called Afrobeats, with an "s" at the end. Artists from Nigeria and Ghana are some of the biggest stars on the planet. Their songs are streamed billions of times. In twenty twenty-one, the Nigerian singer Burna Boy won a Grammy. Afrobeats is now a [[global]] sound.
MAYA: Our third track tonight goes back to the roots. Bright, bouncing guitars, a busy little shaker, and a groove that makes it physically impossible to sit still in your car seat. Please keep your hands on the wheel. I'm [[a big fan of]] this one. It's called "Lagos Sunday".

# Synthwave
[bgm synthwave.13 0.14]
MAYA: Right, the sun is going down, the lights are coming on, and that means it's time for track number four. And this one is what I call proper night-driving music.
MAYA: It's a style called synthwave. And it's a funny one, because it sounds like the nineteen eighties, but it isn't. Most of it was made in the two thousands and twenty tens, by people who grew up watching eighties films and playing eighties video games, and missed that sound.
MAYA: So they took the old synthesisers, the big drums with lots of echo, the bright, shiny melodies, and they made new songs that sound like the soundtrack to a film that was never made. A film with a fast car, a city at night, and a hero in sunglasses who never speaks.

MAYA: Synthwave got a big boost in twenty eleven, from the film "Drive", which is about, yes, a man who drives at night and doesn't talk much. The soundtrack was full of this sound, and suddenly lots of people wanted more of it.

# Lo-fi
[bgm lofi.15 0.13]
MAYA: "Rush Hour Swing". Beautiful. Okay, we're nearly home. One more song.
MAYA: And for the last one, I want to finish with a sound that a lot of you probably know, even if you don't know its name. If you've ever studied late at night with headphones on, or tried to concentrate in a noisy office, you might have listened to it.
MAYA: It's called lo-fi hip hop. Lo-fi means "low fidelity", which is a technical way of saying the sound is deliberately not perfect. A bit soft, a bit fuzzy, with a little crackle in the background, like an old vinyl record.
MAYA: The beats are slow and relaxed. The chords are warm and jazzy. And there are no words, so it doesn't fight with your thoughts. It helps a lot of people [[concentrate]].
MAYA: There's a famous channel on YouTube that has been streaming this music twenty-four hours a day, for years.
